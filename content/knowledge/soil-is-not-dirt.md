"Dirt" is what you wash off your hands. **Soil** is a slow-working city of
life processing yesterday's death into next season's growth. The difference
is worth knowing.

## What a handful contains

A single teaspoon of healthy agricultural soil holds:

- about 1 billion bacteria,
- several hundred metres of fungal threads,
- a small population of springtails, mites, and the occasional heroic earthworm.

More individual organisms than there are humans on Earth, living, eating and
excreting inside the budget of a dessert spoon.

## The three horizons

Soil is arranged in rough layers:

- **Topsoil** — dark, loose, biologically intense; the engine of most plant life.
- **Subsoil** — paler, denser, mineral; salts and clay leach down and settle here.
- **Parent material** — bedrock and leftover geology, slowly dissolving.

Each horizon is the result of the layers above it — water, roots, burrows and
time carrying material downward.

## Why it matters for a knowledge-gathering habit

Soil formation is famously slow: it can take centuries to form a centimetre of
topsoil, and one proper erosion event to remove it. That asymmetry — slow
builders, fast destroyers — is a useful secular way to think about soil,
biodiversity, and any deep capital we rely on.

> The top few centimetres of the earth are, per gram, among the most crowded
> habitats there are. Everything we grow stands on a city we can't see.

## A field acquaintance

Dig a small hole in wet grass. Run your thumb along the dark top layer. That
rough, crumbly, root-shot horizon — that's the part doing a hundred jobs at
once: drainage, storage, anchorage, and a dining room for billions.
