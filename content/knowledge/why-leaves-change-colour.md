Everyone learns that leaves "lose their green" in autumn. The truth is
stranger and more beautiful: the green was masking colours that were there
all along.

## The short answer

As days shorten, a tree stops producing chlorophyll. It degrades faster than
it can be replaced, and as the green recedes, two other pigment families are
exposed:

- **Carotenoids** — orange and yellow. These were simply hiding under the green, doing photosynthesis-adjacent work all summer.
- **Anthocyanins** — red and purple. These are actually *synthesised new* in autumn, like a suncream for the fragile, half-working leaf.

## Why red, though?

A leaf mid-senescence is sloppy at handling excess light. Bright, cold autumn
days produce surplus energy that can damage the cells that are trying to
salvage nutrients back into the tree. Anthocyanins soak up some of that extra
light — a chemical awning. That's why the reddest years tend to be the ones
with warm days and chilly nights: the leaf needs protecting for longer.

## Shaking the hand of a pigment

The molecules at the centre of this — chlorophyll — are a bright green ring
of carbon, nitrogen and magnesium. Each ring catches a photon and hands the
energy on. When the tree decides the game is up, it pulls the magnesium out,
and the ring becomes a dull grey-brown atom assembly instead. The brown you
see later is largely these wrecked magnesium-free rings.

> The colour you see in October was always in the leaf. Autumn just turns the
> lights down in stages.

## Field method

To watch it happen, pick one tree and photograph the same branch once a
fortnight through September and October, ideally at the same time of day.
The sequence — from green, through lime, gold, to open-weave bareness — is
slower than photographs suggest and faster than anyone remembers.
