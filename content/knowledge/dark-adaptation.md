Go from a lit room into a dark field and at first you can barely see. Twenty
minutes later you pick out trails, faces in shadow, and faint detail you
couldn't reach before. That's dark adaptation, and it runs on an exact,
usable timeline.

## Two kinds of light meters

Your retina runs two systems:

- **Cones** adapt in about 5–10 minutes, and handle colour and bright detail — they're the daytime crew.
- **Rods** are built for dimness but adapt far more slowly, roughly 30–40 minutes, going roughly a 100,000-fold more sensitive than daylight vision.

The shift between them — the reason the night sky *gains* stars the longer you
look — is visible for minutes at a stretch, not instantly.

## Red light is a courtesy to yourself

Rods ignore the red end of the spectrum almost entirely. If you use a red
light or red screen dimmed low, the rods carry on adapting while you read a
map. The classic red-light trick trades away a little colour accuracy for a
large measure of preserved night vision.

## Practical numbers

- Blinking lights and phone screens reset the clock in parts — a bright swipe at minute 35 sets you most of the way back to zero.
- Peripheral vision uses more rods; looking slightly away from a faint object makes it easier to see. Astronomers call it averted vision.
- Cadmium-free lamps aside: after an hour of true darkness your sensitivity is near maximum, and further gains are marginal.

> Dark adaptation is the cheapest astronomy instrument you own, and it's ruined
> every time you answer a message.

## A note for photographing the night

That same timeline is why night photography sessions plan a "settle" period:
peg the tripod, cover the screen, then spend the first 20 minutes shooting
bright targets while your own adaptation catches up with the landscape.
