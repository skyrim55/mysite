Reading weather in three clouds

2 min read · May 22, 2025

Forecasts are layers of trusty machines and tempered guesses. But the sky itself is a working instrument, and three cloud types cover most practical questions about the next twelve hours.

1. Cumulus — fair weather, with agency

Puffy, flat-bottomed, cotton-topped afternoon clouds. They form on rising thermals and dissolve by evening.

  • Growing vertically means strong updrafts — expect heavier showers and possible thunder by late afternoon.
  • Flat and small means steady, pleasant weather.

Rule of thumb: if cumulus tops are taller than they are wide, the air is unstable and it will storm. Equal width or wider — you’re fine.

2. Stratus — the blanket

Grey and uniform, barely any texture, sitting low. Stratus is a softened sky. Everything under it is muted: light, sound, mood.

  • Getting lower and darker means drizzle, then rain.
  • Lifting and tearing at the edges means clearing.

Stratus is also most of what people call “overcast”, and it’s the only cloud likely to produce that flat, eggshell-light photographers avoid at midday.

3. Cirrus — the telegram

High, thin, wispy. Cirrus is made of ice crystals and rides the edge of weather that is genuinely distant.

  • Widening into long hooks and banners usually precedes a warm front passing within ~24 hours — clouds fill in, thickening, then rain.
  • Static thin marks that don’t expand suggest the pattern is not coming your way.

Cirrus is the early-warning system; by the time the stratus blanket arrives, the rain was settled hours ago.

Read the sky in order: cirrus tells you what’s developing, stratus tells you what’s here, cumulus tells you what’s possible.

One honest caveat

Mountains and coasts break all three short cuts. Locally trapped air makes its own rules, and that’s exactly why local knowledge beats global forecasts when you’re walking a valley in a mountain climate.
