How fog forms, and when it doesn't

2 min read · September 18, 2025

Fog is not a failed cloud. It is a cloud that decided to sit down. The physics is identical to a cloud a kilometre up: air reaches 100% relative humidity and water condenses onto microscopic particles.

The two main recipes

  1. Radiation fog — calm clear nights. The ground radiates heat away and cools the air above it below the dew point. This is your classic valley fog that burns off by mid-morning. It needs a temperature inversion: cool air trapped under warmer air.
  2. Advection fog — warm wet air crossing a cold surface. Sea fog off cold currents works this way. It can last for days because it isn’t relying on the overnight cooling of the ground; the front keeps feeding it.

When fog won’t form

Even with damp air, fog needs two things: sufficient moisture and condensation nuclei. Completely clean air can sit well above 100% humidity without condensing — supersaturated. In practice dust, pollen, sea salt and industrial aerosols are always present, so it condenses readily. But pathologically clean air delays it and skews the conditions.

Why it forms so early in the morning

The lowest air temperature of the day is right around sunrise, after a full night of the ground doing radiative astronomy. So the classic fog window is 05:00–09:00 — exactly when photographers are supposed to already be there.

Fog is a ground-level inspection of yesterday’s weather: how much heat the ground stored, how wet the air got, and how the wind behaved overnight.

Reading your own valley

Next clear evening: watch where fog collects. It pools in the lowest, calmest, most sheltered spots first — precisely where cold air drains downhill. That drainage is a real, predictable flow, and you can learn your local landscape’s cold air rivers by paying attention twice a year.
