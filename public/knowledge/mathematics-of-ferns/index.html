The quiet mathematics of ferns

2 min read · July 30, 2025

Look closely at a fern frond and you’ll see that each leaflet is a smaller, recurved copy of the whole frond. This self-similarity is what makes ferns the classic textbook example of a recursive system in nature.

Growth as a set of rewrite rules

A frond doesn’t grow from a single plan. It grows by repeatedly applying the same small instruction: tendrils appear at intervals, and each tendril grows as a new start.

In computing, this is an L-system — a set of rules applied over and over, each pass doubling the complexity. Start with a single line, apply the frond rule, apply it again, twenty times, and you get a structure indistinguishable from a real fern.

Why this is “fractal” but without the hype

Real ferns aren’t true fractals — the self-similarity runs out after a handful of levels, twig by twig. But the principle — a small rule amplified by repetition — explains why a fern that fits in a pot and a fern that covers a hillside are built the same way.

The other famous spiral

Then there’s phyllotaxis: leaves, petals, seeds and floret spirals arranged at angles related to continued fractions. Pine cones and sunflowers follow spirals based on the golden ratio not out of aestheticism, but because it’s the most efficient packing that lets every new bud appear as far as possible from the previous one. It’s a spacing algorithm wearing a beautiful coat.

You cannot find a single gene that “draws a fern”. You can find the tiny per-cell rules whose repetition happens to draw one.

Try it at home

Draw a straight line. Add a small curving stub at intervals along it. Now add the same pattern to each stub. Stop when the page feels fern-like. Welcome to recursion — you now share a mental process with a plant that has no brain.
